Can you time the market?

Market timing means moving between stocks and cash to dodge the falls and catch the rises. These simulations use real history to ask four questions: how often would a timer need to be right, what does being out on the wrong months cost, how have simple timing rules done side by side, and does spreading a lump sum over time help?

Educational simulations, not advice. Every number here is hypothetical and computed on past data. Nothing on this page predicts future returns or recommends buying, selling or timing anything.

1. How often would you need to be right?

Imagine a timer who, at the end of every month, guesses whether stocks or cash will do better next month and is right with probability p. Each simulated timer makes its own random mistakes. The chart shows what share of timers end up with more money than simply holding stocks.

Simulation

Context: Sharpe (1975) studied switching once a year between stocks and cash equivalents (1929–1972) and concluded, as reported by later authors, that a timer needs to be right roughly 7 years out of 10 to gain (secondary sources; the original text was not available to us). The simulation here switches monthly and uses your chosen data.

3. Simple timing rules, side by side

Each rule decides at the end of a month, with data up to that month only, whether to hold stocks or cash next month. All rules start on the same month (set by the rule that needs the longest history) and pay the same cost per switch.

What this does and does not show

  • Real investors also face taxes on every switch and the temptation to switch at the wrong moment. Dichev (2007) found that investors’ actual, dollar-weighted returns were lower than the buy-and-hold returns of the same markets, because of when money flowed in and out.
  • The rules shown are textbook versions applied to one index; see the strategy cards for how they differ from the papers.
