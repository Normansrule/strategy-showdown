Is the best backtest a fluke?

Try enough settings of any rule and one of them will look good on past data by luck alone. This page runs every setting of one strategy on the same months, picks the winner the way a researcher would, and measures how often that winner disappoints on months it was not chosen on. That share is the probability of backtest overfitting (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu 2017).

Synthetic data: these results say nothing about real markets. The returns are random numbers. The synthetic market does have a built-in positive average return, so settings that stay invested longer can win in both halves for that reason alone; beyond that, every difference between settings is luck. For real history, use the desktop app or a local copy (see the Showdown page, step 1).

How it is computed

For one split: n* is the setting with the best in-sample Sharpe ratio; ρ is its rank (1 = worst) among the N settings on the out-of-sample months; ω is that rank as a fraction; λ is its logit.

PBO is the share of all C(S, S/2) splits in which the in-sample winner ranks in the bottom half out of sample. With pure noise it is about 50% (slightly more with an odd number of settings); a genuinely better setting pushes it towards 0.

The panel above also shows the deflated Sharpe ratio of the best setting over the full period, which discounts it for the number of settings tried (Bailey & López de Prado 2014). Neighbouring settings are highly correlated, so counting each one as a separate try overstates the number of independent tries and makes that discount conservative. Exact steps, including how ties and leftover months are handled: ENGINE_SPEC §8.

How firmly this is confirmed. The steps follow Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017), Sec. 2, as summarized publicly and implemented in the R package pbo; the full paper could not be re-read while building this page, so edge cases (ties, λ = 0) are labelled “secondary source” on the Sources page. Two independent implementations (JavaScript here, Python in the tests) agree to 1e−9 on every split of the tested sweeps (S = 6 to 16, synthetic test data).

Source: Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017), Journal of Computational Finance 20(4); Bailey & López de Prado (2014).
