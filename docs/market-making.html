Market making, simulated

A market maker always offers two prices: a bid (the price at which it will buy) and an ask (the price at which it will sell). It earns the gap when both sides trade, and loses if the price moves against the stock it is left holding. Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008) worked out where to put the two quotes. This page re-runs the simulation from their paper.

Simulation, not a backtest. Everything here happens in the paper’s stylized market: the price moves up or down by a fixed step, and a quote gets filled at random, with a probability that falls the further the quote is from the mid price. Market making cannot be honestly backtested on monthly (or even daily) price bars, because whether a quote is filled depends on the full order book, the queue, and who else is trading, and none of that is in bar data.

Our simulation vs the paper’s tables

The paper reports 1000 sessions for each γ (Tables 1–3). Monte Carlo results vary with the seed, so small differences are expected. Differences of roughly two standard errors or less are consistent with the paper.

The two equations

Reservation price, Eq. (8)/(29). s = mid price, q = inventory (shares), γ = risk aversion, σ = volatility of the price per unit time, T − t = time left in the session. With long inventory (q > 0) the maker values the stock below the mid, so it quotes lower.

Total spread (ask minus bid), Eq. (30). k measures how quickly the chance of being filled falls as a quote moves away from the mid. The first term shrinks as the session ends; the second is the spread a maker would charge with no inventory risk.

A note on reading Eq. (30): our copy of the paper’s text was garbled at this point. The reading shown, with γσ²(T − t) as the first term, is the one that reproduces the paper’s own average spreads (1.49, 1.35 and 3.02 for γ = 0.1, 0.01, 1). A version with 2γσ²(T − t) gives 1.69, 1.37 and 5.02 instead. Settings used for every run: s₀ = 100, T = 1, σ = 2, dt = 0.005, q₀ = 0, k = 1.5, A = 140 (paper Sec. 3.3).

Source: Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008), Quantitative Finance 8(3). See also the strategy card.
