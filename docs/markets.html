How markets move

Stock prices wiggle in ways that look random, but not purely random. Researchers have catalogued a handful of patterns that show up in almost every market, the “stylized facts” (Cont 2001): fat tails, calm and stormy spells, sharper falls than rises. This page fits six textbook models of monthly returns to the same history, simulates each one hundreds of times, and checks which facts each model can and cannot reproduce.

Which model reproduces which fact?

Each model is fitted to the real returns by maximum likelihood, then simulated. ✓ means the real value lies inside the middle 90% of the model’s simulated histories; ↑ or ↓ means the real value is above or below that range, so the model rarely produces it. Lower AIC and BIC mean a better fit after penalizing extra parameters.

How to read this honestly

  • All six models are deliberately simple and describe returns month by month. None of them knows anything about earnings, interest rates or the economy, so none can tell you what happens next.
  • “✓” only means a model is not contradicted by that one statistic over this history. Each check uses a 90% range, so even a correct model is expected to miss about one of the ten checks by chance.
  • With Shiller’s prices, which his notes describe as monthly averages of daily closes, the lag-1 autocorrelation and the 12-month variance ratio are pushed up by the averaging itself: a pure random walk averaged within each month shows a lag-1 autocorrelation of about 0.25 (Working 1960; checked by simulation in this project’s tests). Use month-end data (the French series) to judge predictability.
